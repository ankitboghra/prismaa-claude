If a doctor has just written "PET-CT whole body" on your prescription, you are probably holding the paper and wondering what it means. This page explains it the way we would explain it to a family member.

## The one-sentence version

A PET/CT is a scan that shows **how the cells inside your body are behaving**, not only what they look like.

That is the whole idea. An X-ray, an ultrasound and a CT scan all show structure — shape, size, position. A PET scan shows *activity*. And in cancer, in dementia, in hidden infection, activity changes long before shape does.

## Two scans, one machine

The name has two halves because the machine does two things in one sitting.

**CT** takes a very detailed three-dimensional photograph of your anatomy. Think of it as a precise map: this is the liver, this is a 9 mm nodule in the upper right lung, this is the lymph node next to the aorta.

**PET** measures which cells are consuming the most energy. It produces a picture that on its own looks like a fuzzy cloud of hot spots with no landmarks.

Laid on top of each other, the two become something neither can be alone: your doctor sees a hot spot *and* knows exactly which structure it sits in.

> A useful way to picture it: a CT is an aerial photograph of a city in daylight — you see every building. A PET is the same city at night — now you see which buildings still have their lights on. Cancer cells keep their lights on.

## Why cancer shows up

Cancer cells divide fast, and dividing costs energy. To pay for it they take in far more glucose — sugar — than the tissue around them.

So we make a molecule that looks like glucose to the body and attach a tiny radioactive tag to it. That is called **FDG**, and it is the tracer used in most PET scans. It is injected into a vein in your arm. Over the next hour your body distributes it exactly as it would distribute sugar: the hungriest cells take up the most.

The scanner then detects where the tag ended up. Hungry cells appear bright. That is all a "hot spot" means.

It is worth knowing that not every bright spot is cancer. Infection, healing after surgery, inflamed joints and even a muscle you used hard yesterday will also light up. Reading a PET scan is largely the skill of telling these apart — which is why the radiologist reporting it matters as much as the machine taking it.

## Why your doctor ordered it

There are usually one of four reasons.

**To find out whether something is cancer at all.** A shadow on a CT might be a tumour, or a scar, or an old tuberculosis lesion. PET often settles it and can spare you a biopsy or an operation.

**To see how far it has gone — staging.** This is the single most common reason. Treatment for cancer confined to one organ is completely different from treatment for cancer that has spread. One whole-body PET/CT answers this in a single pass instead of six separate tests.

**To check whether treatment is working.** A tumour can shrink and still be alive; it can also stay exactly the same size and already be completely dead. Size alone cannot tell you. Metabolic activity can — often months earlier.

**To check for recurrence.** After treatment, PET distinguishes harmless scar tissue from disease that has returned.

## What actually happens on the day

1. **Check-in and preparation, about 15 minutes.** You arrive having fasted for six hours — plain water is allowed and we want you to drink it. We check your blood sugar with a finger-prick and go through your history.
2. **The injection, two minutes.** One needle prick in the arm. You will feel nothing from the tracer itself — no warmth, no taste, no allergic reaction. It is not a contrast dye.
3. **The waiting hour.** You rest quietly in a private room while the tracer distributes. Stay still and relaxed; working muscles absorb tracer and blur the picture. Most people doze off.
4. **The scan, about 15 minutes.** You lie on a padded table that moves slowly through a wide, short ring. It is open at both ends. Nothing closes over your face. There is no pain and almost no noise.
5. **Home.** Eat normally. Drink plenty of water to flush the tracer out.
6. **The report, usually within 24 hours.**

Plan for around two and a half hours in the centre in total. The scan itself is the shortest part of the day.

## The questions people are too polite to ask

**Will it hurt?** No. One needle prick, and nothing after that.

**Is it like an MRI tunnel?** No. The PET/CT ring is short and wide and open at both ends. Your head is clear of it for most of the scan. People who cannot tolerate an MRI almost always manage a PET/CT comfortably.

**Will I be radioactive?** For a few hours, mildly. The tracer decays quickly and washes out in your urine. Normal contact with adults the same day is fine. We simply ask you to avoid prolonged close contact with pregnant women and infants until the next morning.

**Is the radiation going to harm me?** The dose is comparable to a few years of the natural background radiation everyone on earth receives from soil, food and cosmic rays. It is a small risk, and it is set against the much larger risk of planning cancer treatment on incomplete information. A newer digital scanner — like the one at Prismaa — needs less tracer for the same image quality, which lowers it further.

**Can I eat before?** No, six hours of fasting. Sugar in your blood competes with the tracer and weakens the scan. Water is not only allowed, it helps.

**I am diabetic.** Tell us when you book. We will give you a slot and specific instructions about your medication and insulin timing. Diabetes does not prevent you having the scan.

## Not only for cancer

PET/CT is best known for oncology, but it is also used to distinguish types of dementia, to locate the origin of seizures in epilepsy that drugs cannot control, to work out whether weakened heart muscle after a heart attack is dead or merely starved — and to hunt down hidden infection in someone who has had a fever for weeks with every other test normal.

## One last thing

If you receive a report you cannot understand, bring it to us and ask. We will sit down with you and your family and go through the images. Nobody should walk out of a diagnostic centre holding a page of words they cannot read.
